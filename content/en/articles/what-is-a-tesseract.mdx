There's a [tesseract](/en/experiments/observatory/tesseract) spinning in this
site's Observatory, and the question I get most isn't how it's coded but
something earlier: **what is a tesseract?** This post explains it without
formulas, ends with a drawing you can make on paper and, at the end, clears up
what it has to do with the one in *Interstellar*.

## The ladder of dimensions

The easiest way to reach a tesseract is to climb one step at a time.

- A **point** has no dimensions.
- Drag the point in a straight line and you get a **segment**: one dimension.
- Drag the segment sideways, at a right angle, and you get a **square**: two
  dimensions.
- Drag the square upward, at a right angle to both sides it already has, and
  you get a **cube**: three dimensions.
- Drag the cube in a direction at a right angle to all three of our space and
  you get a **tesseract**: four dimensions.

The last step is the one we can't picture, because there's no fourth direction
to point at. But you can reason about it exactly like the earlier ones, and
that's enough to count it.

## How many vertices, edges and faces it has

Every time you drag a shape, its vertices double (the ones at the start and the
ones at the end) and each original vertex leaves a trail, which is a new edge.

| Shape | Dimensions | Vertices | Edges | Square faces | Cubes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Segment | 1 | 2 | 1 | — | — |
| Square | 2 | 4 | 4 | 1 | — |
| Cube | 3 | 8 | 12 | 6 | 1 |
| Tesseract | 4 | **16** | **32** | **24** | **8** |

So a tesseract has 16 vertices, 32 edges, 24 square faces and 8 cubic cells.
Four edges meet at every vertex, one per dimension, just as three meet at every
corner of a cube.

## Why it looks like a cube inside a cube

When you draw a cube on paper, you aren't drawing the cube: you're drawing its
two-dimensional shadow. The back face comes out smaller than the front one,
even though on the real cube they're the same size. That's perspective.

A tesseract does the same thing one step up. What we see on a screen, or in the
Observatory, is its three-dimensional shadow: two cubes, one inside the other,
with their eight corners joined. **The small cube isn't smaller**: it's the one
farther away in the fourth direction, like the back face of the drawn cube. The
six pieces joining them, which look like truncated pyramids, are also perfect
cubes seen in perspective.

That's why, when a tesseract rotates in four dimensions, it seems to turn
itself inside out like a sock: the inner cube grows, comes out and becomes the
outer one. Nothing is deforming. What changes is which of its cubes is closest
to us in the direction we can't see.

## Flatland: how you'd see a tesseract passing by

In 1884 Edwin Abbott published *Flatland*, a novel about beings who live on a
plane. If a cube passed through their world, they wouldn't see a cube: they'd
see a square appear out of nowhere, stay for a while and vanish, because they
only perceive the cube's slice through their plane.

If a tesseract passed through our space, the same would happen to us: we'd see
a cube appear out of nowhere, stay and vanish. That analogy, thinking about how
a lower-dimensional being would see our world, is the most useful tool for
thinking in four.

## How to draw a tesseract by hand

You need paper and a ruler. There are two ways; this is the easier one.

1. **Draw a cube.** A square, an identical one shifted diagonally, and join
   the four corners. That's 8 vertices and 12 edges.
2. **Draw a second identical cube**, shifted in another direction (say, to the
   right and a bit down). Don't let it overlap the first one completely.
3. **Join each vertex of the first cube to the matching vertex of the
   second.** That's 8 more lines.
4. **Count:** 12 + 12 + 8 = 32 edges and 16 vertices. If you get those
   numbers, it's a tesseract.

The second way is the cube inside the cube: draw a large cube, a smaller one
inside it, and join its eight corners to the large one's. That's the
perspective view I explained above, the same one the Observatory shows. Both
drawings are the same object seen from different places.

If you want to see how it's done in code, with the tesseract actually rotating
in four dimensions, I walk through it in
[How I drew a 4D tesseract in Three.js](/en/blog/4d-tesseract-in-three-js).

## Where the name comes from

The word was coined by the British mathematician Charles Howard Hinton in 1888,
in his book *A New Era of Thought*. It comes from Greek: "four rays", for the
four edges leaving each vertex. It's also called a **hypercube**, though that
word is more general: a hypercube can have any number of dimensions, and the
tesseract is the four-dimensional one.

If you've ever seen Salvador Dalí's *Corpus Hypercubus* (1954), the cross in
the painting is an unfolded tesseract: its eight cubes laid out in our space,
just as a flattened cardboard box is the flat pattern of a cube.

## And Interstellar's tesseract?

In *Interstellar*, after falling into Gargantua, Cooper ends up inside a
structure the film calls the tesseract: his daughter Murph's bedroom repeated
in every direction, each copy at a different moment. There, time becomes a
place you can move through, and Cooper reaches Murph by nudging things in the
past with gravity.

It isn't a geometric hypercube like the one in this post: it's an image
inspired by it, to stand for more dimensions than we can see. Kip Thorne, the
physicist who advised the film, explains the idea in his book *The Science of
Interstellar*. It keeps the essential part of the real tesseract: what we see
is always the shadow of something with more dimensions.
