## Cuerpo (inglés)

The header of my portfolio's Education page is a still image of an alien
ocean. A fragment shader makes it move: https://jonasjavier.dev/en/education

It's not Three.js, just a raw WebGL2 context, one full-screen triangle and
the image as a texture. The sky is never touched: anything above the horizon
(24% of the image height) returns the texture as is.

Below it, the shader rebuilds a sea plane in perspective, `z = 1/(0.9·d + 0.1)`
with `d` the depth under the horizon, and sums three wave trains rolling
toward the camera plus a noise ripple. Each train returns height and slope
analytically, with deep-water speed:

```glsl
vec3 wave(vec2 w, vec2 dir, float k, float amp, float t, float phase) {
  float p = k * (dot(dir, w) - SPEED * inversesqrt(k) * t) + phase;
  return vec3(amp * sin(p), amp * k * cos(p) * dir);
}
```

It never draws a wave. It offsets the texture lookup by the slope
(refraction) and lifts it by the height, so all the foam and highlights in
the image stay and just start breathing. A sun from the right adds Blinn
glints, and sparkles only where the image already has foam.

My first try was a 2D canvas shifting 3 px rows by depth only. Every band
moved as one block. It looked like jelly, not water.

The part I care about most is that it doesn't cost the page anything. It
waits for `requestIdleCallback` (context + shaders were ~200 ms on a
GPU-less Chrome), polls `KHR_parallel_shader_compile` instead of reading
`COMPILE_STATUS` right away (the same pattern took a ~380 ms task on another
page down to 80–250 ms), runs at 30 fps, and stops off screen and in
background tabs.

What isn't solved: the shader's swells ignore the swells painted in the
image. Stare at one crest and you'll see the water move while the crest
doesn't.

Question: has anyone driven a refraction field like this from the image
itself (a depth or flow map) without it turning into a full video pipeline?
