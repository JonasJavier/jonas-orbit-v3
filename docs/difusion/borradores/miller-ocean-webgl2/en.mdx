My [Education](/en/education) page is Miller's planet: an ocean with no
shore. At the top there's a large image of that sea, and the sea moves.
Swells roll toward you, the light on the right catches the faces turned to
it, and the sky stays still.

It isn't a video. It's the same image, refracted every frame by a WebGL2
fragment shader. This post is how it works and, more than that, how I made
the water cost the page nothing: not the first paint, not the arrival from
another page, not the battery while you're not looking at it.

## The first version was jelly

The first version used a 2D canvas. It sliced the water into 3 px rows and
shifted each row along a sine wave. On paper, waves. On screen, no.

My verdict was short: the motion just didn't look right. It took me a while
to see why. It wasn't speed or amplitude. Each row's phase depended **only
on depth**. A whole horizontal band moved as one block, side to side and up
and down, and the image wobbled like jelly. The field didn't vary across
the screen and had no perspective. No amount of tuning was going to fix
that: the model was wrong.

## One triangle and one image

The current version has its own WebGL2 context, one texture (the image) and
a single triangle that covers the screen. No Three.js here: a full-screen
pass doesn't need it.

```glsl
void main() {
  vec2 uv = uOffset + vUv * uCover;
  float d = (uv.y - HORIZON) / (1.0 - HORIZON);
  if (d <= 0.0) { outColor = texture(uPhoto, uv); return; }
  // …
}
```

`HORIZON` is `0.24`: the image's horizon sits at 24% of its height. `d` is
depth below that line. If a pixel is in the sky (`d <= 0`), the shader
returns the texture untouched and stops. The sky is never modified.

`uCover` and `uOffset` reproduce the `<img>`'s `object-fit: cover`. The
shader computes its own crop from `naturalWidth`, so the water lands exactly
where the still image was.

## A sea in perspective

To stop the jelly, each pixel has to know where it sits on an actual sea
plane, not just how far down the screen it is. So the shader rebuilds a
perspective plane:

```glsl
float z = 1.0 / (0.9 * d + 0.1);
vec2 w = vec2((uv.x - 0.5) * z * 1.6, z) * 23.0;
```

`z` grows toward the horizon. Near the camera one unit of sea covers many
pixels; far away, very few. On that plane I add three wave trains rolling
toward the camera, one head-on and two oblique, plus a noise ripple dragged
in the same direction.

```glsl
// Tren sinusoidal con derivadas analíticas: (altura, dh/dx, dh/dy).
// La velocidad decrece con la raíz del número de onda, como en aguas profundas.
vec3 wave(vec2 w, vec2 dir, float k, float amp, float t, float phase) {
  float p = k * (dot(dir, w) - SPEED * inversesqrt(k) * t) + phase;
  return vec3(amp * sin(p), amp * k * cos(p) * dir);
}
```

(The comments are in Spanish because the repo is.) Two things matter here.
Speed scales with `1/√k`, the deep-water dispersion relation: short waves
travel slower than long ones. And the function returns the height **and its
slope** together, as analytic derivatives. No neighbour samples needed to
know which way the water tilts.

Because the plane is in perspective, distant crests come out small and slow
and near ones big and fast. That is exactly what the 2D version lacked.

## Refract, don't draw

The shader doesn't paint waves. It moves the image that's already there.

```glsl
// Refracción: la pendiente desvía la muestra; la altura la levanta.
vec2 shift = f.yz * 0.0017 * reach + vec2(0.0, f.x * 0.005 * reach);
vec3 color = texture(uPhoto, uv + shift).rgb;
```

Each pixel samples the texture slightly offset by the water's slope at that
point, the way light bends through a tilted surface. Height lifts it a bit
more. You keep every detail of the image —the foam, the silver highlights—
and it starts to breathe.

Two terms control the reach. `smoothstep(0.0, 0.16, d)` fades the offset
out near the horizon, so there's no seam where water meets sky. And
`pow(d, 1.5)` makes the effect grow toward the foreground.

Light goes on top. The sun is low on the right. Faces of the water turned
toward it gain a little brightness, faces turned away lose some. Crests get
Blinn highlights, plus brief sparkles that only show up where the image
already has foam: a dark patch stays dark.

## Tuned in pixels per second

Water shaders are easy to tune by eye and just as easy to ruin. So I pinned
speed to something measurable: the main train moves about 70 px/s near the
bottom edge, with 600 px of water, and slows with perspective toward the
horizon.

Then I measured it with Playwright at 1440 px wide: three screenshots 0.5 s
apart. Between frames, 12–13% of the water pixels change, with a mean
difference of 5.5 levels. The sky sits at 0.005 —practically nothing,
which is the point. A strip of six frames 0.2 s apart shows crests moving
down and to the left with a steady pace and no banding.

## Keeping the water off the main thread

This is the part that took the most work, and the part nobody sees.

**The image comes first.** It's in the server-rendered HTML and it's the
page's LCP. It's preloaded from the `<head>`, one version per screen size:
a phone only shows a centre strip of the landscape, so it gets that strip
pre-cropped, 53 KB instead of 195. The shader computes the same crop on
either one.

**The water waits for idle.** Creating the WebGL context and compiling the
shaders takes about 200 ms on a GPU-less Chrome. It used to happen as soon
as the image loaded, in the middle of hydration. Now it waits for
`lib/after-load-idle.ts`: the page has loaded and the browser is idle
(`requestIdleCallback`, with a 2 s cap). Until then you see the same still
image with the same crop, so nothing jumps.

**Shader compilation doesn't freeze the arrival.** Compiled the usual way
—`compileShader`, then immediately read `COMPILE_STATUS`— the driver
finishes the work right there, inside the commit that paints the new page.
I measured it on the other piece that uses the same pattern, the cockpit on
my Contact page: a ~380 ms task right at the page change. Now the status
isn't read until the program is ready:

```ts
const parallel = gl.getExtension("KHR_parallel_shader_compile") as ParallelShaderCompile | null;
let frame = 0;
const poll = () => {
  if (parallel && !gl.getProgramParameter(program, parallel.COMPLETION_STATUS_KHR)) {
    frame = requestAnimationFrame(poll);
    return;
  }
  frame = 0;
  done(Boolean(gl.getProgramParameter(program, gl.LINK_STATUS)));
};
```

With `KHR_parallel_shader_compile` the driver compiles on another thread
and I poll once per frame. Without the extension, the read still moves to
the next frame, out of the commit. On Contact the longest task went from
378 ms to 80–250.

**It only draws when you can see it.** The loop runs at 30 fps, not 60: the
sea is slow and you can't tell the difference. An `IntersectionObserver`
stops it when the header scrolls away, and `visibilitychange` stops it in a
background tab. The canvas matches its box at DPR ≤ 1.5 and never goes past
2048 px wide.

**Never two contexts drawing.** Behind every page of the site lives the 3D
map scene. On Education that scene sleeps, covered by the page, so the
water is the only thing drawing.

## When it can't move

The sea follows the site's single motion switch, in the tray at the bottom
right. It has no button of its own. Turn motion off and the canvas is
removed, leaving the still image. When it goes, the WebGL context is
released: browsers allow only a few per tab, and a still image has no
business holding one.

No WebGL2, or a lost context, ends the same way: the image stays still and
there's no control on screen. A "pause" button on an image that can't move
would be a lie.

Without JavaScript, the page is the image, the text and the documents. All
the content is in the HTML.

And it's tested. The end-to-end test for Miller counts `drawArrays` calls on
the water canvas over several frames: that it draws, that it stops with
motion off, off screen and in a background tab.

## What isn't solved

The waves know nothing about the image. The shader's swells travel over the
swells already in the picture without following them. At reading distance
it works; stare at one specific crest and you'll see the water move while
the crest doesn't.

And the page doesn't load faster because of this. The water stopped
competing with hydration, but the Education LCP didn't move in that
measurement (3.65 ↔ 3.83 s). What went down was main-thread blocking. The
LCP is still the image, and the work there is about bytes, not shaders.
