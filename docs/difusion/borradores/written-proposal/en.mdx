In my [guide to hiring a freelance developer](/en/blog/how-to-hire-a-freelance-web-developer-dominican-republic)
I said the first thing to insist on is a written proposal, and I gave a short
list of what it should contain. This post opens that list up: what I actually
write in each part, with examples from my own projects, and what goes wrong
when a part is missing.

It works if you're hiring me. It works just as well if you're hiring someone
else. A good proposal doesn't depend on who writes it.

## The proposal is the first deliverable

The way I work has four steps: conversation, proposal, build and launch. The
proposal comes second on purpose. First I learn the problem, who will use the
product and what success looks like. Then I write it down, before anything
starts.

I don't treat it as paperwork. It's the first thing I deliver, and you can
review it the way you'd review a screen: if you read it and don't recognize
your business, I got something wrong, and this is the cheapest moment to fix
it. Fixing a paragraph costs an email. Fixing a screen that's already built
costs days.

## The problem, in one sentence

The first section isn't about technology. It states the problem the project
solves, in one or two sentences you'd sign off on.

On [OMSTA](/en/projects/omsta), the system behind a travel agency, the problem
was that sales, money and accounting lived in separate places: the same data
got typed in several times and the balances didn't add up. The goal fit on one
line: capture a sale once, and get the payment, the invoice, the journal
entries and the reports out of it without retyping anything.

That sentence is the referee for the whole project. When a new idea shows up,
the question is whether it moves you toward that goal. Without it, every new
idea feels equally urgent.

## What gets built

The second section is the scope: what will exist when we're done. I write it
in the language of your business, not mine. Not "a bookings CRUD with a REST
API," but "an agent creates a booking, records the payment, and accounting
verifies and closes it."

What helps me is writing it by people and by tasks:

- **Who logs in**, and with which role. OMSTA has five base roles, and each
  one sees different things.
- **What each of them does**, as verbs: create, collect, verify, export.
- **Where they do it**: on the web, on a phone, or both.
- **What the end customer walks away with**: an order, an invoice, a message.

The tech stack goes at the end, in a few lines. It matters to whoever
maintains the system later, but it doesn't decide whether the project solves
your problem. If a proposal spends more space on the stack than on what you'll
be able to do with it, it was written to impress you, not to reach an
agreement.

## What's out of scope

This section prevents most arguments, and it's the one people skip. Anything
that isn't written down as out of scope, somebody will assume is included.

On [Delicaté](/en/projects/delicate-4-0), an online store for handmade soaps,
what was out of scope was half the design. There are no online payments, no
customer accounts and no order dashboard: you build your order on the site and
finish it on WhatsApp, because that's how the business sells. That's why the
cart total says "estimated." Buying requires zero accounts. None of that is an
oversight. It's written down, and the published case study has a section of
its own for it: "What it doesn't have."

OMSTA has the same kind of line on taxes. The system prepares, validates and
downloads the reports for the DGII, the Dominican tax authority. A person
files them. If that isn't in writing, someone expects the system to "do the
taxes" and gets a surprise on filing day.

Writing down what's excluded isn't refusing to build it. It puts it in plain
sight so you can decide on it calmly, maybe as a second phase.

## Milestones you can open

A "three months" timeline tells you nothing until the last day. I'd rather
split the project into milestones, each one something you can open and try on
a real link, from your laptop or your phone.

For each milestone, the proposal says:

- **What you'll be able to do** when you open it. Not "40% done," but "you can
  create a booking and see it in the list."
- **When**, in weeks or with a date.
- **What it depends on from your side** to land on time.

The order gets decided here too. I built OMSTA in layers: bookings and clients
first, then everything that moves money, then the accounting, and the tax
reports last. Each layer rests on the one before it, and each one is usable
before the next one exists.

## Budget: it depends on the scope

This is the question everyone asks first, and the honest answer is always the
same: it depends on the scope. That's not a dodge. It's the reason the
proposal exists. Until the scope is written down, any number is a guess, and a
low guess today turns into an argument tomorrow.

What the proposal does need to say about money:

- **The total** for the written scope, not for a rough idea.
- **How it's split**: what you pay, and when.
- **Which milestone each payment is tied to.** If every payment matches
  something you can open and test, you know what you're paying for.
- **What the price doesn't cover**: domain, hosting, third-party services.

If someone quotes you a fixed price in their first message, having barely
asked you anything, that isn't a price. It's a request for trust.

## What I need from you

No project depends only on the developer. The proposal should spell out what I
bring, what you bring, and by when: copy, photos, logins, accounts and
decisions.

Two examples from my own work. The OMSTA mobile app is already set up for iOS,
but the iPhone version is waiting on the business's Apple Developer account.
That's not something I can open on their behalf. And before Delicaté is
announced, there are checks that depend on the business: the delivery and
privacy policies, and a full order tested on Android and on iPhone.

Neither one is a hidden delay. Both are written down, each with an owner. When
a dependency is in the proposal, a slipping date has a cause we can both see.
When it isn't, a slipping date looks like somebody's fault.

## Who owns what, and what happens after launch

The proposal states who owns each piece when we're done: the domain in your
name, hosting in your accounts or with owner access, the code yours once it's
paid for, and a list of credentials so someone else can pick up the work if
it ever comes to that. I went through this in more detail in the
[hiring guide](/en/blog/how-to-hire-a-freelance-web-developer-dominican-republic).

It also says what happens after launch. I take care of the release and the
domain, and I stick around after delivery. What needs to be in writing is the
detail: what happens with a bug in the first few weeks, and how small changes
are handled later on.

## When something changes

Something will change. When you see the first milestone on your phone, a
better idea than the one on paper almost always shows up. That's good: it's
the whole point of putting every step on a real link.

What can't happen is a change agreed on in a voice note that nobody writes
down. My rule is simple: a change gets written the same way the proposal was.
What's added, what's removed to make room or pushed back, and how it affects
the milestones and the budget. If one line of the scope changes, the lines
that depend on it change too.

An honest note. I don't have a proposal template published for you to
download, and I won't show my clients' proposals: they carry their budgets and
their data. What I can show is the result, the published case studies, where
you can see what was built and what was left out. If you want to see what
yours would look like, the shortest path is to ask for one.

## Try it

Before you hire anyone, take the last proposal someone sent you, or the idea
in your head, and look for these parts: the problem in one sentence, what gets
built, what's out of scope, milestones you can open, money tied to those
milestones, what they need from you, and how changes work. Whatever is missing
is what you'll end up arguing about later.
