The home page of this site is a still system around a black hole. Gargantua
sits in the center with five bodies around it: the Endurance, the Ranger,
Miller's planet, Edmunds' planet and a tesseract. All six are inspired by
*Interstellar*, and you can look at each one up close in the
[Observatory](/en/experiments), full screen and in 3D.

They aren't downloaded models or videos. Everything is computed in the
browser with WebGL, Three.js and custom shaders. This post is the tour: what
the six share, the one decision that made each of them work, and what's still
open.

## One light for six bodies

There isn't a single `THREE.Light` in the scene. The only light source is
Gargantua's accretion disk, at the origin, and every other body is lit from
there. The values live in one fragment shader shared by every body in the
system:

- **Key:** the amber of the disk, `vec3(1.0, 0.78, 0.52)`. The side facing it.
- **Fill:** a very low blue. It's the sky, not a second lamp.
- **Cold rim:** closes the silhouette against the black.
- **Terminator:** a `smoothstep` over `n·l` plus relief. The lit crescent.

That gives you consistency for free. If the Endurance is to the right of the
black hole, its left side is the one that glows. Nobody has to remember to
place it.

One rule applies to all six: the **bloom-off test**. I switch off bloom and
emissives, and every body still has to read. Silhouette, volume, material and
hierarchy. If a body loses its identity when the glow goes away, it isn't
finished.

## Gargantua: four views instead of an orbit

[Gargantua](/en/experiments/observatory/gargantua) isn't drawn. A shader
follows light ray by ray around the black hole: 190 steps per pixel on the
normal tier and 340 on the deep one. How it's built has
[its own post](/en/blog/how-i-built-a-black-hole-in-webgl), and the physics
behind it has [another](/en/blog/interstellar-black-hole-gargantua-explained).

What matters here is an Observatory decision. You can rotate the other five
bodies freely with the mouse. Not Gargantua. It has four fixed views:
**Cinematic, Lens, Disk and Shadow**. Each one studies one thing: the
composition, the photon ring, the Doppler asymmetry and the edge of the black.

Why no free orbit? The final quality is paid for by accumulating the image
over eight sample positions, and dragging throws that accumulation away. Then
there's the bigger reason: with a free orbit, someone would end up looking at
the black hole from an angle nobody framed. The camera never goes below 9° of
elevation, because below that you're chasing a frame from the film at the
expense of your own composition.

## The Endurance: twelve modules in four draw calls

The [Endurance](/en/experiments/observatory/endurance) had to have weight. It
had to read as engineering, not as a pretty silhouette against the light.

The first problem was scale. At system size the details vanish, and the ship
flattened into a bright smudge. Adding parts didn't fix it. The cavities did:
dark gaps between modules and light breaking across the facets.

It's twelve modules in four groups. Arms, thermal panels and nozzles are
merged into **four draw calls**. The engine plume lives inside the emissive
material and costs zero extra calls. The maneuvering thrusters moved from the
barrel to the rim of the ring, because on the barrel they didn't read.

The latest pass made it properly white and gave it **24 lit windows**: three
per inhabited module, none on the cargo bays. Warm, fixed and dim, lower than
the beacons. Interior light, not a signal. It's the cheapest hint that someone
lives inside.

## The Ranger: a hull in one piece

The [Ranger](/en/experiments/observatory/ranger) just changed shape. Its
fuselage used to be a rounded box plus a cone plus a cap. Now it's **one
lofted surface** of nine stations with smooth normals: wider, flatter, with a
belly flatter than its back.

The plating went from cream to gunmetal. The reason is the light: the Ranger
gets Gargantua almost from behind, at 153°. A light hull washes out from that
angle. A dark one lives on amber edges, a specular sweep and the reflection of
the disk.

That reflection doesn't use a cubemap. This system's environment can be
written as a formula: a huge amber ring around the origin, hugging the orbital
plane, and black everywhere else. The shader reflects the view ray against
that direction and that's it. No textures.

## Miller: an ocean is a behavior

[Miller's planet](/en/experiments/observatory/miller) went through four
revisions that made it darker each time. Every step had a correct argument.
The result didn't: on screen it was a blue rock.

What I was missing is that **"ocean" isn't an exposure level, it's a
behavior**. A path of light that moves, glints, clouds drifting at a different
speed from the water. All of that reads just as well on a bright planet.

The swell is three wave scales with analytic gradients (the derivative of a
sine is a cosine, so it costs no extra sample), each moving at a different
speed. And here's the trap. I raised the speeds and saw nothing. I was looking
at the wrong number: the speed that matters is **pixels per second**. Miller
spins on its axis at 0.05 rad/s, which is 2.35 px/s at its equator. The waves
moved between 0.5 and 2.5 px/s. They were as slow as the surface carrying
them, or slower, so they looked painted on.

The rule I kept: on a spinning body, an animated field doesn't exist until its
speed is several times the spin. Here, four to six times.

## Edmunds: geology, not texture

[Edmunds' planet](/en/experiments/observatory/edmunds) had the opposite
problem: too much texture. Lots of blotches of the same size, all equally
important. It looked like a decal on a sphere.

The cause was specific. The four mineral provinces were decided by very
narrow `smoothstep` gates, 0.05 to 0.065 wide, over a four-octave fbm noise.
The fine octaves added about ±0.15 of ripple: three times the width of the
gate. The fine noise was drawing the edge of each province, not the large
shape.

The fix was to change the field that decides the geography. It's now three
low-frequency directional waves: smooth by construction, so a wide gate gives
a large mass. The fbm stayed, but only to perturb the border. And color and
shading now come from the same terrain. Before there were two: one painted
and one lit.

## The tesseract: hierarchy, not exposure

The [tesseract](/en/experiments/observatory/tesseract) is the only one that
isn't an object from the film as such. It's a four-dimensional hypercube:
sixteen vertices rotating across four planes and projected in perspective. A
light runs along its 32 edges in an Eulerian circuit. Four draw calls and one
shared material.

The first version was mathematically correct and impossible to read: 32 glass
edges the eye couldn't sort into depth. More exposure didn't help. The
opposite did: each vertex publishes its depth in the fourth dimension, and the
far cell gets thinner and dimmer. The step-by-step is in
[its own post](/en/blog/4d-tesseract-in-three-js).

## The Observatory: looking and measuring are two things

All six live in the same instrument. At first it had everything switched on at
once and felt heavy. Looking at a specimen and measuring it are two different
activities, so they became two modes: **Observe**, the resting state (the
body, its name and nothing else), and **Study**, which opens the console.

In Study there are three gestures, and each one changes exactly one thing:

- **Drag** moves the camera. It changes the face you see and the light angle.
- **Light** moves the body around the origin. Same face, different light.
- **Figure** spins the body on its own axis. Different face, same light.

The third one came last, and it's the one that answers the useful question:
what's on the other half of this planet? When you drag, the other half always
arrives lit differently, and you can't tell geography from lighting.

## What isn't solved

Gargantua's free orbit is still out. Nobody has shown that the image survives
the manipulation, or that every reachable angle keeps the composition. Maybe
one day. Not today.

And quality depends on the machine. With a real GPU there's 4x MSAA across the
whole chain; with software rendering (a machine without graphics
acceleration) there isn't, and the ships' edges come out as stairs. That's why
I only judge the bodies on a real GPU: a software capture can't tell you
whether a pass worked.
