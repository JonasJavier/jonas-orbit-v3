Everyone remembers the scene. The crew lands on Miller's planet knowing that
every hour down there is seven years on Earth. A wave costs them a little over
three hours, and when they get back to the ship, Romilly has been waiting for
twenty-three years.

Miller is one of the six bodies I built for
[the Observatory](/en/experiments/observatory/miller) on this site, and what
people search about it isn't how it's made. It's this: **could that really
happen?**

Short answer: yes, under very strict conditions. This post goes through them,
with numbers you can check.

## The short answer: gravity slows clocks down

Since Einstein, we know time doesn't pass at the same rate everywhere. Near a
large mass, a clock runs slower than one far away. The clock isn't broken.
Time itself is slower. It's called **gravitational time dilation**.

Nobody on Miller would notice anything. Their watch, their pulse, their
coffee all run normally. The difference only shows up when you compare: back
on the ship, which orbits much farther from Gargantua, the crew finds out that
years went by outside.

To get one hour against seven years you need three things at once: a huge
black hole, a planet orbiting extremely close to it, and a black hole spinning
almost as fast as physics allows. Drop any one of them and the scene doesn't
exist.

## Not science fiction: your phone corrects for it

Time dilation sounds exotic, but it's measured every day. GPS satellites orbit
about 20,000 km up, where Earth's gravity is weaker. Up there their atomic
clocks run ahead by about 45 microseconds a day compared with clocks on the
ground. Because they also move fast, special relativity slows them by about
7. The net drift is about **38 microseconds a day**.

That sounds like nothing, but light travels about 11 kilometers in that time.
Left uncorrected, your phone's position would drift by kilometers every day.
The satellite clocks are tuned before launch precisely to cancel it.

In 1959, Robert Pound and Glen Rebka measured the effect in a 22.5-meter
tower at Harvard: light going down arrived with slightly more energy than
light going up. Today's best optical clocks can tell apart two heights less
than a meter from each other.

What changes on Miller isn't the physics. It's the scale.

## How big is "one hour, seven years"?

Seven years is about 61,000 hours. So on Miller time runs about **61,000
times slower** than on Earth.

For comparison: at Earth's surface, its own gravity slows clocks by less than
one part in a billion. On Miller the clock barely moves: for every second that
passes there, almost seventeen hours pass on Earth.

You can't get that with a big planet or a heavy star. Not even the surface of
a neutron star, the densest thing short of a black hole, comes close. You have
to be sitting almost on top of a black hole's event horizon, the edge nothing
comes back from. And that's where the problem starts.

## Why a non-spinning black hole isn't enough

You can't orbit just anywhere near a black hole. If it doesn't spin, there's a
minimum distance below which no orbit is stable: three times the radius of
the horizon. Any closer and the planet spirals in.

At that innermost orbit, adding up gravity and the planet's own speed, time
slows by a factor of about 1.4. In other words: **one hour on the planet would
be 1.4 hours outside**. Nowhere near seven years.

This one hits close to home. My
[Gargantua simulator](/en/experiments/observatory/gargantua) uses the geometry
of a non-spinning black hole, because tracing rotation in real time in a
browser was too expensive (I explain it in
[the physics of Interstellar's black hole](/en/blog/interstellar-black-hole-gargantua-explained)).
Around the Gargantua in my simulator, Miller's planet couldn't exist. That's
when I understood spin isn't a detail of the film. It's the plot.

## Gargantua's spin: what makes it possible

A spinning black hole drags space around with it. The faster it spins, the
closer the innermost stable orbit gets to the horizon, and at maximum spin it
reaches it. There, time dilation can get as large as you like.

Kip Thorne, the physicist who advised the film, ran those numbers. In his
book *The Science of Interstellar* (2014) he says that to get one hour per
seven years, Gargantua has to spin within about **one part in a hundred
trillion** of the maximum. That's practically the limit relativity allows.

Mass matters too, for a different reason. Near a small black hole, the
difference in gravity between one side of the planet and the other would tear
it apart. Near one of about a hundred million suns, like Gargantua, those tidal
forces are gentle enough around the horizon for a planet to stay in one piece.
I go into that in
[Gargantua vs. Sagittarius A* and M87*](/en/blog/gargantua-vs-sagittarius-a-and-m87).

## The kilometer-high waves

Miller's waves have a reputation as the most exaggerated thing in the film: a
wall of water more than a kilometer tall that comes around every so often.
Thorne explains them with tides. Gargantua pulls on the near side of the
planet harder than on the far side, and that difference, huge at that
distance, deforms the whole ocean.

On Earth the Moon raises the sea by centimeters or a few meters. On Miller
the black hole is so close and so heavy that the same physics builds
mountains of water. It's one of the least disputed parts: if you accept the
orbit, the waves come with it.

## Where the film stretched it

Interstellar doesn't break physics on Miller, but it takes it to the edge, and
it's worth saying where.

- **A spin like that is unlikely.** Thorne himself showed in 1974 that a black
  hole feeding from a gas disk brakes itself: the radiation it swallows keeps
  it from going past about 99.8% of maximum spin. Gargantua sits way above
  that. The film makes it fit with a cold, nearly empty disk, but it's not
  what we'd expect to find in nature.
- **Getting in and out isn't free.** Dropping into an orbit that deep and
  climbing back out takes an enormous amount of energy. The film doesn't stop
  to explain it, and it's the part that leans most on fiction.
- **A habitable planet wouldn't have a good spot there.** Light from the rest
  of the universe would reach Miller strongly blueshifted and concentrated. A
  cold, calm ocean between waves is a choice of the story.

## Questions people ask me

**How long were they on Miller's planet?** A little over three hours. At seven
years per hour, that's the twenty-three years Romilly spent alone on the ship.

**Why did Romilly age and the others didn't?** Because he stayed on the ship,
in a much wider orbit, where time runs almost as it does on Earth. The ones
who landed lived three hours; he lived twenty-three years.

**Is time dilation real?** Yes. It's measured with atomic clocks on planes, on
satellites and in labs, and GPS corrects for it every day. What nobody has
seen is one this extreme: we don't know of any planet next to a black hole.

**Could a planet like Miller exist?** The laws of physics don't forbid it,
but it needs a black hole spinning faster than we expect to find in nature,
and a planet that survives that neighborhood. Possible on paper, unlikely in
the sky.

**What's the black hole near Miller's planet called?** Gargantua, the same one
as in the rest of the film. What it is and why it looks the way it does is in
[the physics post](/en/blog/interstellar-black-hole-gargantua-explained).
