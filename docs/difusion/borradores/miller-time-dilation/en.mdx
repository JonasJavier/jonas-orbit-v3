Everyone remembers the scene in *Interstellar*. The crew drops onto Miller's
planet, a shoreless ocean, knowing that every hour down there costs seven
years back on Earth. A giant wave slows them down. By the time they make it
back to the ship, the crewmate who stayed in orbit has aged for years.

After "does Gargantua exist?", it's the question that brings the most people
to my [Gargantua simulator](/en/experiments/observatory/gargantua): **why is
one hour seven years on Miller's planet?** I answered it in three lines at the
end of
[Interstellar's black hole, explained](/en/blog/interstellar-black-hole-gargantua-explained).
This is the full answer, with the math, and with a confession: in my own
simulator, Miller couldn't exist.

## The short answer

Near a huge mass, time runs slower. Not the clocks. Time itself. It's called
**gravitational time dilation**, and Einstein's general relativity predicts it.

Miller's planet orbits very, very close to Gargantua's event horizon. Close
enough that its time runs tens of thousands of times slower than Earth's. For
that orbit to be possible you need two things at once: a black hole of about a
hundred million solar masses, and a spin almost as fast as physics allows.
Lose either one and there's no Miller.

## Time runs slower near a mass

This sounds like science fiction, but you rely on it every day. GPS satellites
carry atomic clocks, and those clocks, farther from Earth, tick slightly
faster than clocks on the ground. If engineers didn't correct for that, GPS
would drift a little more every day until it couldn't find you. On Earth the
effect is tiny. You can measure it. You can't feel it.

The key point: **nobody feels their own time slowing down**. On Miller, a
clock reads one hour after one hour. Your heart beats normally. A
conversation takes as long as it takes. The difference only shows up when you
compare. Bring two clocks back together after they spent time in different
places, and one of them has ticked far less than the other.

That's exactly what happens in the movie. The crew gets back to the ship
having lost nothing. They lost everything that happened outside.

## How much slower: running Miller's numbers

Seven years is about 61,000 hours. So the movie's rule means time on Miller
runs roughly **61,000 times** slower than on Earth.

Put another way:

- Every **second** on Miller is about **17 hours** on Earth.
- Every **minute** is about **six weeks**.
- Every **hour** is seven years.

You don't get that factor by being "near" a black hole. You get it by
practically touching one. Time dilation grows slowly as you approach, then
shoots up in the last stretch right above the horizon. To reach 61,000,
Miller has to orbit in a razor-thin band just outside the edge nothing comes
back from.

That's where the problems start.

## Getting closer isn't enough: orbits have a floor

A planet can't hover above a black hole. It has to orbit, the way Earth orbits
the Sun. And around a black hole there's a distance below which **no orbit is
stable**.

For a black hole that doesn't spin, that innermost stable orbit sits at three
times the horizon radius. Anything inside it spirals in. I ran into the same
limit with the accretion disk: gas can't orbit stably inside those three
radii.

I ran the numbers for that case. A planet on the closest stable orbit around a
non-spinning black hole would have its time only about **1.4 times slower**
than someone far away. One hour there would be about 1.4 hours outside.
Nowhere near seven years.

So a black hole like the one in my simulator won't work. You need a different
one.

## Why Gargantua has to spin near the limit

A spinning black hole drags space around with it. One consequence is that the
innermost stable orbit moves much farther in: the faster it spins, the closer
to the horizon a planet can orbit without falling.

Push the spin close to the maximum physics allows, and that orbit nearly
touches the horizon. In that band, time dilation can get as large as you want.
That's the trick Kip Thorne, the physicist who advised the film, used. He
didn't invent new physics. He picked a spin so close to the maximum that a
factor of 61,000 fits inside general relativity.

That's why I say the movie pushed physics as far as physics allows on Miller,
but no further. A real black hole spinning that fast would be extraordinary.
It isn't forbidden.

## Why the tides don't tear the planet apart

There's a third problem. A planet that close to a black hole feels **tidal
forces**: gravity pulls harder on the near side than on the far side. Here,
that difference with the Moon moves the oceans. Near a small black hole, a few
solar masses, it stretches a planet until it breaks, long before it reaches
any interesting orbit.

The counterintuitive part: at the horizon, tides get **gentler the bigger the
black hole is**. That's why Gargantua weighs about a hundred million Suns. It's
what Miller needs to orbit right against the horizon and stay in one piece.
Thorne explains it in *The Science of Interstellar*: the mass wasn't picked
for spectacle. It was picked so the planet would survive.

Put the three pieces together and you have the movie's plot translated into
physics:

1. **Extreme time dilation** → the planet has to hug the horizon.
2. **A stable orbit hugging the horizon** → the black hole has to spin near
   the maximum.
3. **A planet that survives hugging the horizon** → the black hole has to be
   huge.

If you want the size comparison with real black holes, it's in
[Gargantua vs. Sagittarius A* and M87*](/en/blog/gargantua-vs-sagittarius-a-and-m87).

## What the movie stretched, and what it left out

I want to be precise here, because it's easy to get carried away.

The story needs a Gargantua that spins almost at the limit. The **image** of
Gargantua on screen, though, was computed with a much lower spin: 0.6 of the
maximum, according to the scientific paper the effects team published with
Thorne in 2015. At the story's spin, the shadow came out flattened on one side
and the disk off-center, and Christopher Nolan and the visual effects
supervisor thought it would confuse audiences. So the movie tells you about
one black hole and shows you a slightly different one.

There's more the film doesn't stop to explain: how a ship slows down enough
to drop into an orbit like that and climb back out, or how much energy that
takes. Thorne covers it in his book. I haven't checked those parts closely,
and I'm not going to quote them from memory. The giant waves have their own
explanation too, and they deserve their own post.

What does hold up is the core idea: with the right mass and spin, one hour can
be seven years.

## In my simulator, Miller couldn't exist

Now the honest part about my site.

The Gargantua in my [Observatory](/en/experiments/observatory/gargantua)
**doesn't spin**. Light follows the geometry of a non-rotating black hole,
Schwarzschild, because tracing a rotating one per pixel in a browser was too
expensive. The disk's gas rotates; space doesn't. From everything above,
around that black hole the best possible orbit gets you 1.4, not 61,000. In
my universe, Miller would have to sit farther out, and its hour would last a
little over an hour.

And on the home page map, the bodies **don't orbit**. Where the six sit around
Gargantua is an art direction call: they stay still on purpose, so the system
reads as places to go, not as a clock.

[Miller has its own specimen](/en/experiments/observatory/miller): a sphere
with a custom shader where swell, light path and glints are computed every
frame. It has three views: **Canonical**, **Mirror** and **Currents**. The
sea keeps moving even when the camera is still.

Here's a detail I like. What you see in the Observatory is the ocean in **its
own** time, the way someone standing on the planet would see it. If the
specimen respected relativity, seen from far away it would look almost
frozen: a wave that takes one second to pass down there would take about 17
hours to cross your screen from the ship. I didn't build it that way, because
a frozen planet tells you nothing about its water. But it's the kind of call
worth saying out loud.
